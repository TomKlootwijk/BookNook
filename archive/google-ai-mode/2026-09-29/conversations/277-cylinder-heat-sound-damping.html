Managing noise, frequency, and vibration in hot, cylindrical systems—like engine cylinders, flue gas pipes, or compressor systems—requires a combination of vibration damping, sound absorption, and thermal resistance. Because low-frequency noises behave differently than high-frequency noises, different materials and mechanisms must be used to effectively silence the system. 
Here is a breakdown of how to control noise and heat in these environments: 

1. Vibration Damping vs. Sound Absorption

Managing sound requires addressing both structure-borne noise (vibrations) and airborne noise (hums and whistles). 
Dynamat
  • Vibration Damping (Low Frequencies): Cylindrical metal components can vibrate heavily, acting like a drum. Damping compounds or Constrained Layer Damping (CLD) mats—such as butyl rubber mats—absorb mechanical energy and turn it into tiny amounts of harmless heat. This targets low-to-mid frequency hums. 
    ABM Insulation +2
  • Sound Absorption (Mid-to-High Frequencies): Open-cell acoustic foams or fiber blankets soak up the sound waves traveling through the air. 
    ABM Insulation +1
  • Thermal Protection: Standard acoustic foams will melt or burn near high heat. You must use specialty thermo-acoustic barriers (like closed-cell insulation rubber foam or high-temp fiberglass sleeves) to protect the damping layer from heat degradation. 
    YouTube·Soundproof Guide +2

2. Muffling the Cylinder or Exhaust (Reactive vs. Absorptive)

If the noise is traveling through an active exhaust flow or duct, a muffler or silencer is needed: 
ATA Mute +1
Muffler TypeBest ForMechanism of Action
Reactive / ChamberedLow Frequencies (50–500 Hz)Uses internal geometry, perforated plates, and resonator chambers to force sound waves to collide and cancel each other out.
Absorptive / Straight-ThroughHigh Frequencies (>1000 Hz)Uses porous, high-heat material (like fiberglass packing) wrapped around a perforated core to absorb sound energy without creating high back-pressure.

3. Real-World Applications

  • For Heat Pumps & Inverter Compressors: Compressors generate a harsh low-frequency rumbling. Wrapping the cylinder in a heavy acoustic compressor jacket (1–5 mm thick) reduces low-frequency tonal noise significantly. Additionally, mounting the unit on polyurethane vibration isolators prevents the vibration from transferring to the floor or walls. 
    Getzner Werkstoffe +2
  • For Engines and Exhaust Hoods: To quiet an engine bay, a multi-layer approach is used. First, a butyl damping mat is rolled onto the metal panels to stop the vibration. Then, a second layer of heat-resistant closed-cell foam or a thermal liner is added to block out high-frequency airborne noise and deflect engine heat. 
    Stinger - Define Your Drive +2
  • For Industrial Flue Gas Systems: Systems like boilers or generators often use hollow-chamber low-frequency silencers. These lack standard fibers (which can burn or clog with soot) and use smooth, acoustically coupled external resonators to damp out deep humming safely.

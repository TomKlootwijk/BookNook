In exact computational models, social choice theory, and distributed architecture, this limitation is formally proven by several core theorems:

### 1. Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem (Multi-Option Paradox)

When a system has **three or more distinct options**, it is mathematically impossible to design a ranked-voting system that reflects the perfect, un-coerced consensus of a community while remaining completely fair.

Formally proved by economist Kenneth Arrow, the theorem states that no voting system can convert individual ranked preferences into a community-wide ranked preference without violating at least one of these deterministic criteria:

* **Non-Dictatorship:** The system cannot just mirror the choice of a single component/individual.
* **Universal Domain:** Every possible combination of preferences must yield a valid, deterministic output.
* **Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA):** If the group prefers Option A over Option B, introducing a third Option C should not flip the preference between A and B.

Because a system cannot satisfy all of these simultaneously, **perfect consensus breaks down the moment you move past 2 options**.

### 2. The Condorcet Paradox (Cyclical Majority)

Even if every single individual unit in a system has completely linear, deterministic, and rational preferences, the *collective* system can become completely irrational and deadlocked.

For example, if you have three voting nodes ( $1, 2, 3$ ) and three options ( $A, B, C$ ):

* Node 1 prefers:  $A > B > C$
* Node 2 prefers:  $B > C > A$
* Node 3 prefers:  $C > A > B$

If you run a majority vote between any two options:

* A majority prefers  $A$  over  $B$  (Nodes 1 and 3)
* A majority prefers  $B$  over  $C$  (Nodes 1 and 2)
* A majority prefers  $C$  over  $A$  (Nodes 2 and 3)

The system output is a continuous loop ( $A > B > C > A$ ). Majority voting logic fails here because it cannot resolve a clear winner without an arbitrary tie-breaker rule, which instantly violates pure determinism.

### 3. Distributed Systems: The FLP Impossibility Result

In computer architecture and distributed networks, the **FLP Impossibility Theorem** (Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson) proves a similar structural limit. It states that in an asynchronous network, if even a single process or node can experience an unannounced failure or "bit shift," it is **impossible to guarantee deterministic consensus** among the remaining nodes. The system must choose between being perfectly deterministic or being guaranteed to finish its calculation.

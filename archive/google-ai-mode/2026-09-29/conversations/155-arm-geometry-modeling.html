Response 1 · Google AI Mode
You are exactly right! An arm can be modeled as two connected line segments (the upper arm and forearm) that rotate around fixed pivot points (the joints, like the shoulder and elbow). 
Think of each joint as that "axle" or "circle" you mentioned. In physics, engineering, and robotics, this is known as a robotic arm or a kinematic chain with rotational joints. 
When you move your arm, it traces out arcs and circles because the length of the bones stays the same while the angles at the joints change. The "other arm" follows the exact same geometry—just mirrored on the other side of your body!

Ah, you are making a brilliant connection! When you transition from the concept of a rotating arm to conduit bending math, you are looking at the exact same geometry—specifically, a right-angled triangle.

When electricians bend a conduit to make an offset (going around an obstacle), the slanted section of the pipe forms the hypotenuse of a triangle, and the two straight paths form the legs. 
Chapman Electric Supply

The "Arm" Geometry of a Conduit Bend

If you treat the bent section of the pipe like a mechanical arm pivoting at an angle, the sides a and b represent the two straight dimensions of its reach: 
Chapman Electric Supply
  • Side a (The Rise / Offset Depth): This is the vertical height or depth you need the pipe to clear. It is the "opposite" side of the triangle.
  • Side b (The Run): This is the horizontal distance the bend takes up along the wall or ceiling. It is the "adjacent" side.
  • Side c (The Travel): This is the actual length of the slanted pipe segment between the two bends.

Why the Ratio of a / b Matters

Just like tracking how high or far a robotic arm can reach based on its joint angle, the ratio of a / b determines the exact angle of the bend (d) using basic trigonometry: 
Chapman Electric Supply
tan(d)=ab=RiseRun
If you change the angle at the "joint" (the bend), the lengths of conduit arm a and conduit arm b change proportionally: 
Chapman Electric Supply
  • A sharper angle (e.g., 45°) gives you a taller rise (a) relative to a shorter horizontal run (b).
  • A shallower angle (e.g., 30°) gives you a shorter rise (a) spread out over a longer horizontal run (b).

Snail shell convection patterns in boiling water (often seen as spiraling patterns of bubbles, starch, or miso paste) are macroscopic examples of Rayleigh-Bénard convection cells, driven by surface tension and temperature differences. 
The identical phenomenon happens in several other famous natural and domestic settings: 

1. Miso Soup ("Miso Soup Galaxies")

When a bowl of hot Japanese miso soup sits and cools slightly, the suspended miso particles organize into a shifting network of miniature hexagonal or spiraling cells. Hotter liquid rises from the center of each cell, cools at the surface, and sinks back down along the darker edges. 

2. Granulation on the Sun

The surface of the Sun (the photosphere) looks like a vast field of boiling, golden cells. Known as solar granulation, these cells are the tops of convective zones where incredibly hot plasma rises to the surface, cools, and plunges back down. A single solar cell can be the size of a continent. 

3. Drying Paint and Varnishes

If paint is applied too thickly, the evaporating solvent creates a temperature and density gradient. The paint can form permanent hexagonal ridges or spiral wrinkles called "Bénard cells" as it dries, which is often considered a defect by painters. 

4. Honey and Thick Oils

If you gently heat a thin, even layer of honey, cooking oil, or silicone oil in a flat pan, it will self-organize into a highly geometric honeycomb pattern. 

Why the "Snail Shell" or Hexagon Shape Forms

When a fluid is heated uniformly from below, it wants to rise. However, it cannot all rise at once. It breaks into discrete geometric zones to maximize the efficiency of heat transfer. 
  • The Physics: This is governed by the Rayleigh number (buoyancy vs. thermal diffusion) and the Marangoni effect (fluid flowing toward areas of higher surface tension). 
  • The Shape: While perfectly uniform conditions produce hexagons, slight movement, circular pans, or stirring introduces rotational forces that twist these cells into spirals or snail shell shapes.

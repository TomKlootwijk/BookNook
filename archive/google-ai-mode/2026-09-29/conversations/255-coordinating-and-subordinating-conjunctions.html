It sounds like you are looking at how coordinating conjunctions and subordinating conjunctions affect verb tenses and timing (especially when using the word "when") in a sentence. 
Here is the quick breakdown of how timing works with both types: 

1. Coordinating Conjunctions (Same Time / Equal Weight)

Coordinating conjunctions (like and, but, or, so) connect two independent clauses that have equal grammatical importance. The actions usually happen sequentially or at the same time, and the tenses typically match. 
  • Example: "The ship hit the reef and it immediately began sinking." (Both actions happen in the same past time frame). 

2. Subordinating Conjunctions (Shifted Time / Main vs. Background)

Subordinating conjunctions (like when, because, although, while) introduce a dependent clause. They establish a clear time relationship, showing which action is the "background" and which is the "trigger." 
When using "when" to talk about a sinking event, the timing depends entirely on the verb tenses you choose: 
Sentence StructureTiming / Meaning
"The captain stayed onboard when the ship was sinking."Simultaneous / Background: One action happens during the ongoing process of the other.
"The captain jumped overboard when the ship sank."Sequence: One action happens immediately after the other finishes.
"The captain had already left when the ship sank."Prior Action: One action was completely finished before the other occurred.

Summary Rule for "When"

  • When + Past Continuous (was sinking) = An action interrupted a longer, ongoing event.
  • When + Simple Past (sank) = One sudden event triggered another.

Ah! You mean a coordinate system and how it tracks time syncing (often called time synchronization or coordination) across multiple coordinates or nodes. 
In physics, engineering, and computing, a coordinate system that includes time is called a spacetime coordinate system or a four-vector (x, y, z, t). 
Here is how coordinate systems and time synchronization connect: 

1. The Core Problem: Why "Sinking" (Syncing) Time Matters

If multiple sensors, satellites, or computers are at different positions (coordinates), their clocks will inherently drift apart due to mechanical limits, network latency, or relativity. To make sense of data, they must coordinate their clocks to a single master time coordinate. 

2. How Time is Coordinated Across Systems

Depending on your field, time synchronization across a coordinate system relies on specific protocols: 
  • Global Positioning System (GPS): GPS satellites use incredibly precise atomic clocks. Your phone receives signals from multiple satellites at different spatial coordinates (x, y, z) and calculates the exact time delay to determine your precise position and sync its internal clock. 
  • Network Time Protocol (NTP) & PTP: In computer networks, devices at different network coordinates use NTP (accurate to milliseconds) or PTP (Precision Time Protocol, accurate to microseconds) to constantly send timestamped messages back and forth, calculating and correcting for delivery delays. 
  • Relativity (Einstein's Spacetime): In physics, observers at different coordinates moving at different speeds will experience time differently (time dilation). To coordinate a system here, physicists use frameworks like Lorentz transformations to translate time coordinates from one moving reference frame to another.

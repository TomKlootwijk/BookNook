There is **no theoretical maximum computable number**, because the set of computable numbers is **countably infinite**, meaning you can always add 1 to any computable number to get a larger computable number.

However, you might be thinking of two related constraints in computer science and mathematics that create "maximums": **physical computing limits** and **computability limits** (the Busy Beaver problem). 

![](../assets/d32708b273c3a53428c0.png)Mathematics Stack Exchange

### 1. The Physical Maximum (Kolmogorov Complexity)

If you interpret "computable" as *"what can actually be calculated or stored within our physical universe,"* there is a strict upper bound.

* The observable universe is estimated to contain roughly **10⁸⁰ to 10⁸⁵ atoms**, and a theoretical storage capacity of no more than **10¹⁸⁵ bits** of information. 

  ![](../assets/d32708b273c3a53428c0.png)Mathematics Stack Exchange
* Any number that requires more than 10¹⁸⁵ bits of raw information to express cannot exist in the universe.
* However, we can bypass this with **compressed representations**. For example,  $10^{10^{100}}$  is a massive number, but it has low [Kolmogorov complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_complexity) because the *program* to generate it is very short ("10 to the power of 10 to the power of 100"). The absolute physical limit applies to numbers with high Kolmogorov complexity—numbers that are completely random and cannot be compressed. 

  ![](../assets/d32708b273c3a53428c0.png)Mathematics Stack Exchange

### 2. The Limit of What We Can Prove to be Computable (Busy Beaver)

A [computable number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable_number) is defined as a real number whose digits can be generated indefinitely by a finite algorithm (a Turing machine). While there is no largest number, there is a limit to how fast these numbers can grow based on what we can mathematically prove. 

![](../assets/7861cff9e1816e7201f0.png)Quora +2

This is best illustrated by the **Busy Beaver game** Σ(n), which asks: *What is the maximum number of 1s a Turing machine with n states can write on a blank tape before halting?*

* For a 2-state machine, Σ(2) = 4.
* For a 5-state machine, Σ(5) ≥ 40,988.
* For a 6-state machine, Σ(6) is at least  $10^{10,572}$ .

The Busy Beaver function grows faster than *any* computable function. Because of the **Halting Problem** (we cannot algorithmically determine if a random program will run forever or eventually stop), the exact values of Σ(n) for larger machines are **non-computable**. 

![](../assets/d32708b273c3a53428c0.png)Mathematics Stack Exchange

* **In pure math:** There is **no maximum** computable number; they span to infinity.
* **In the physical universe:** The maximum exact, uncompressible number is capped by the **10¹⁸⁵-bit storage limit** of the observable universe.
* **In algorithmic limits:** The largest numbers we can intentionally compute are bounded by our inability to solve the **Halting Problem** for complex programs.

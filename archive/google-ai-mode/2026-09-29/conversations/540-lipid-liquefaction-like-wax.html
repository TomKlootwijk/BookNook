The phrase "lipid liquefaction like candle wax" usually refers to one of two main concepts, depending on whether you are looking at it through a medical pathology or a biochemical lens. 

1. The Medical Context: Fat Necrosis (Acute Pancreatitis)

In medicine, "candle-wax drippings" is a classic gross pathology term used to describe fat necrosis. 
PrepLadder
  • The Process: During severe abdominal injuries or conditions like acute pancreatitis, damaged pancreatic cells release digestive enzymes called lipases into the surrounding fat tissue. 
    PrepLadder
  • The Liquefaction: These lipases rapidly break down (liquefy) triglycerides into free fatty acids. 
    PrepLadder
  • The "Candle Wax" Appearance: These released fatty acids combine with calcium in the body through a process called saponification (soap-making). This creates chalky, white, firm deposits on the surface of abdominal organs that look exactly like drippings from a burning candle. 
    PrepLadder

2. The Biochemical Context: Phase Transitions of Waxes

From a purely chemical standpoint, waxes are a specific category of lipids. 
Khan Academy·Khan Academy +1
  • Molecular Structure: Unlike regular fats, natural or lipid-based waxes consist of long-chain fatty acids linked to long-chain alcohols.
  • Liquefaction: At room temperature, these lipid chains are tightly packed into a rigid, plastic solid. When heat is applied (such as lighting a candle), the thermal energy overcomes the intermolecular bonds. The solid lipid undergoes a physical phase change, melting into a low-viscosity liquid.

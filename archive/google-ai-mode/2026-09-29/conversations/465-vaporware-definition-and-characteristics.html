vaporware
Vaporware is a computer software, hardware, or electronic product announced to the general public that is severely delayed, cancelled, or never actually released. 
Wikipedia +1

Key Characteristics

  • Premature Announcement: Products are heavily advertised months or even years before development is ready. 
    Wikipedia +1
  • Missing Details: Very few concrete updates or technical milestones are shared during the delay. 
    Wikipedia
  • Strategic Intent: Companies sometimes announce vaporware intentionally to prevent customers from buying a competitor's active product. 
    Wikipedia +1
  • Modern Evolution: In recent years, the term has expanded to include AI tools or cloud software with exaggerated, non-existent capabilities. 
    YouTube·Marcus Harris Software & Tech Attorney

Famous Examples

  • Historical: The IBM System/360 Model 91 was famously accused by the U.S. Justice Department of being announced years early to stall rival sales. 
    Wikipedia
  • Eventual Releases: Products like Bluetooth, 3G, and Windows Vista spent years trapped in delayed or vaporware status before finally launching. 
    Wikipedia
  • Video Games: Titles like Beyond Good and Evil 2 and the Prince of Persia: The Sands of Time Remake have faced multi-decade development cycles or indefinite pauses. 
    YouTube·Nerdthusiast +1

Response 2 · Google AI Mode
The IBM System/360 Model 91 incident is widely considered one of the earliest and most infamous examples of corporate vaporware strategy in history. 
Wikipedia +1

The Backstory: IBM vs. Control Data Corporation

In 1964, a much smaller competitor called Control Data Corporation (CDC) shocked the tech world by releasing the CDC 6600, which was recognized as the world's first true supercomputer. It completely outperformed anything IBM had at the time. Infuriated by losing the industry lead, IBM's president, Tom Watson Jr., famously issued what became known as the "Janitor Memo," questioning how a company with only "34 people, including the janitor" could beat IBM. 
www.computerhistory.org +1

The Strategy

To stop customers from abandoning IBM to buy the CDC 6600, IBM quickly announced a massively powerful upcoming addition to their mainframe lineup: the System/360 Model 91 (initially teased as the Model 92). They claimed it would be faster and more efficient than CDC’s machine. 
Wikipedia +2
The tactic worked perfectly. Because many major laboratories and universities preferred staying with IBM, they froze their purchasing plans and opted to wait for the Model 91. Sales for the CDC 6600 dried up significantly. 
Wikipedia +1

The Fallout and Lawsuit

The problem was that the Model 91 didn't actually exist at the time of the announcement; it was entirely theoretical. IBM struggled heavily with its development, and by the time they actually shipped the machine years later, only about 15 units were ever built, and the product line was quickly withdrawn. 
Wikipedia +3
This became the basis for a landmark antitrust lawsuit. In 1968, CDC sued IBM, accusing them of using "knowingly false announcements" to illegally monopolize the market. IBM eventually settled out of court, paying CDC a massive settlement valued at hundreds of millions of dollars in cash and assets. The U.S. Justice Department also launched its own massive anti-monopoly case against IBM in 1969 citing these actions. 
Wikiwand +3
While the word "vaporware" wasn't coined until the 1980s, tech historians always point back to this 1960s chess move as the absolute blueprint for the term.

technology
/tɛkˈnɒlədʒi/
Technology is the application of scientific knowledge, tools, and methods to solve practical problems and improve human life. You can explore the comprehensive background and history of the concept on Wikipedia. 
Britannica +2

Core Definitions and Origins

  • Etymology: The word comes from the Greek roots technē (meaning art, craft, or skill) and logia (meaning study or knowledge).
  • Practical Purpose: It moves beyond pure theory into the creation of physical hardware (like tools, machines, and computers) and intangible systems (like software and algorithms). 
    Wikipedia +2

Major Types of Technology

  • Mechanical Technology: Uses physical components like wheels, levers, gears, and engines to move or transform energy.
  • Electronic Technology: Relies on electric circuits and power to run devices such as home appliances, televisions, and computers.
  • Information Technology (IT): Focuses on the creation, processing, storage, and exchange of electronic data using networks and computers.
  • Medical and Biotechnology: Applies science to diagnose, treat, and prevent diseases using tools like MRIs, pharmaceuticals, and ventilators.

Response 2 · Google AI Mode
The year 1965 was a massive turning point for Artificial Intelligence (AI), shifting the field from purely theoretical concepts into functional software. It marked the transition into the "Golden Age" of early AI, driven by immense academic optimism and strategic government funding. 
The year featured several major historical breakthroughs: 

1. Invention of the First "Expert System": DENDRAL

In 1965, AI researcher Edward Feigenbaum and geneticist Joshua Lederberg at Stanford University began building DENDRAL. It is widely celebrated as the world's first expert system. 
Tableau +1
  • The Tech: Instead of using general problem-solving logic, DENDRAL was hardcoded with the specific domain knowledge of human experts (chemists) to map unknown organic molecules from mass spectrometry data. 
    Wikipedia +1
  • The Impact: It proved that AI could match human experts in a specialized field, laying the groundwork for the rule-based commercial AI boom of the 1980s. 
    The Hundred-Page Language Models Book +1

2. Creation of ELIZA (The First Chatbot)

Though fully demonstrated at MIT over the following year, Joseph Weizenbaum wrote and developed the core program for ELIZA in 1965. 
Forbes +1
  • The Tech: It used basic natural language processing (NLP) to match text patterns and mimic a Rogerian psychotherapist. It would cleverly flip a user's statement back into a question (e.g., User: "My head hurts." ELIZA: "Why do you say your head hurts?"). 
    MindStudio +1
  • The Impact: It was the ancestor of modern chatbots and large language models. It famously revealed the "ELIZA Effect," demonstrating how easily humans anthropomorphize and form emotional attachments to machines. 
    MindStudio

3. Founding of SAIL

John McCarthy, who originally coined the term "Artificial Intelligence" in 1955, founded the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (SAIL) in 1965. Backed heavily by the US Department of Defense's DARPA, SAIL—alongside labs at MIT, Carnegie Mellon, and the University of Edinburgh—became one of the global pillars of early computer science and robotics innovation. 
Wikipedia +2

4. Overconfidence & Prophecies

The successes of 1965 triggered intense overconfidence among researchers: 
  • The 20-Year Prediction: AI pioneer Herbert Simon famously declared in 1965 that "machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do." This extreme optimism eventually contributed to a massive drop in funding (the "AI Winter") when those promises weren't met.
  • The Intelligence Explosion: Mathematician I.J. Good published a paper in 1965 predicting an "ultraintelligent machine" that could continuously redesign itself. This was the first formal description of the "intelligence explosion" or technological singularity that shapes modern AI safety debates today.

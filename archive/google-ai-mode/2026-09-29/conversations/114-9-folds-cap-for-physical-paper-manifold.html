Response 1 · Google AI Mode
The physical limit—or "cap"—for folding a two-dimensional physical paper manifold (a flat sheet of paper) 9 times in a single direction is determined by Britney Gallivan's paper-folding loss equation. While conventional myth long claimed that 7 or 8 folds was an absolute physical impossibility, Gallivan proved that any number of folds is achievable provided you possess a piece of material with a high enough length-to-thickness ratio (L/t). 
Fermat's Library +4

The Governing Mathematical Cap

When folding a material of thickness t repeatedly in a single direction, material is exponentially lost to the semicircular creased boundaries (the radius and creep sections). The strict minimum bounding length L required to complete n folds is governed by: 
ResearchGate +2
L=πt6(2n+4)(2n−1)

Physical Requirements for 9 Folds

For n = 9, the required length-to-thickness ratio (L/t) evaluates to exactly 138,060.4. 
  • Standard Printing Paper (t ≈ 0.1 mm): To successfully achieve 9 folds, your starting paper manifold must be at least 13.81 meters (45.3 feet) long. A standard A4 or Letter sheet is far too short, which is why manual attempts typically fail at fold 6 or 7. 
    Live Science +1
  • Structural Reality: At 9 folds, you are dealing with 2⁹ = 512 individual layers of paper compressed together. Even if you have the length, the sheer structural rigidity of 512 stacked layers usually requires mechanical assistance (such as a hydraulic press) to compress the unyielding material at the boundaries.

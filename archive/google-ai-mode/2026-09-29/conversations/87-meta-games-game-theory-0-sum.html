A zero-sum game in game theory is a mathematical representation of a situation where one participant's gain or loss is exactly balanced by the corresponding losses or gains of the other participants, resulting in a total net change of zero. 
Wikipedia

Core Concepts of Zero-Sum Games

  • Strict Opposition: The interests of the players are completely opposed; there is no room for cooperation, mutual benefit, or a "win-win" scenario. 
    Play For Thoughts +1
  • The Minimax Theorem: Proven by John von Neumann, this theorem states that in a two-player, zero-sum game with mixed strategies, the maximum a player can guarantee themselves (the maximin) equals the minimum the opponent can force them to accept (the minimax). 
    Tradermath
  • Nash Equilibrium: Every finite, two-player zero-sum game has at least one Nash equilibrium when players use mixed strategies (randomizing their choices based on specific probabilities). 
    Brilliant
  • Real-World Examples: Classic examples include chess, poker, matching pennies, and elections (where one candidate's win is the other's loss). It is often misapplied to macroeconomics or stock markets, which function as non-zero-sum because wealth can be created or destroyed through growth. 
    Wikipedia +2

Yes, it is exactly the same John von Neumann. 
You are thinking of the von Neumann bottleneck, a foundational concept in computer science that literally dictates the mechanical limits of modern technology. 
Wikipedia
The connection between his work in game theory and his work in computer design centers around how data flows—and gets stuck—within a system: 

1. What is the literal "von Neumann Bottleneck"?

When von Neumann designed the blueprint for modern computers (the von Neumann Architecture) in 1945, he split the machine into two main parts: the CPU (the brain that does the calculations) and the Memory (where data and instructions live). 
TechTarget +1
To connect them, he used a single hardware pathway called a bus. Because this bus can only carry one piece of data or one instruction at a time, the incredibly fast CPU is constantly forced to sit idle, waiting for data to travel back and forth from the slower memory. 
Medium·Hannah S +2

2. How it restricts "Literal Technical Growth"

Computer processors have grown exponentially faster over the decades, but the physical speed of moving data across that shared bus has not kept pace. Today, this bottleneck is the primary barrier to technical growth in several massive industries: 
Scribd +1
  • The AI Scaling Problem: Modern AI and Deep Learning workloads require moving billions of parameters (weights) back and forth. Because of the von Neumann bottleneck, roughly 90% of the energy consumed by AI data centers is wasted just moving data through the bottleneck, not actually processing it. 
    IBM Research
  • The Physical Limit: We have reached a point where making CPUs faster is pointless unless we change how they are built, because they spend most of their time choked by data delivery. 
    Medium·Hannah S +1

3. The Mirror to Game Theory

There is a poetic loop between his two famous concepts. In game theory, zero-sum matrix games assume a world of strict, sequential rules where resources are fixed. Similarly, his computer architecture forces operations to happen strictly one at a time, sequentially. 
GeeksforGeeks
To break through this literal technical bottleneck, modern engineers are forced to build "Non-von Neumann" architectures—like quantum computing, neuromorphic chips (which mimic human brains by putting memory and processing in the same exact spot), and massive parallel computing systems. 
IGI Global +2

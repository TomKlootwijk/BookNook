Here is how the defense mechanisms stack up against a raider's leverage strategies: 

The Anatomy of a Poison Pill

When a corporate raider crosses a pre-determined ownership threshold (historically 15% to 20%, but heavily activist-targeted companies sometimes drop this trigger to 10% or 15%), the pill detonates. 
Management uses two primary variations to dilute the raider out of existence: 
  • The Flip-In Pill (Most Common): The moment the raider hits the trigger threshold, every shareholder except the raider receives the right to buy additional shares of the target company at a massive discount (usually 50% off).
    • The Result: The market is flooded with new, cheap shares. The raider's hard-earned 3.5% or 10% stake is instantly diluted down to a fraction of its voting power, and the cost to buy a controlling interest skyrockets. 
  • The Flip-Over Pill: This triggers after a successful merger. It allows the target company's shareholders to buy shares of the acquiring company at a steep discount.
    • The Result: It threatens to dilute the raider’s own parent company structure, poisoning the deal from the inside out. 

How Raiders Outsmart the Pill

As a former raider, you know the pill isn't completely invincible. Because a board cannot legally keep a poison pill in place forever without breaching their fiduciary duty to maximize shareholder value, raiders evolved their tactics: 
  • The Proxy Fight + Pill Removal: Raiders rarely cross the trigger line on purpose. Instead, they use their sub-threshold stake to launch a proxy campaign to replace the board of directors. The goal? Install a new board that promises to redeem (cancel) the poison pill, clearing the path for the takeover. 
  • The Net Operating Loss (NOL) Pill: Some companies use a loop-hole trigger as low as 4.9% under the guise of protecting tax assets (NOLs). This is the ultimate counter to the stealth 3.5% strategy, forcing raiders to make their intentions public much earlier.

1. Willard Van Orman Quine (Philosopher & Logician)

Born in 1908 and passing away in 2000, W.V.O. Quine spent his career primarily at Harvard University. He is celebrated for his "naturalism"—the view that philosophy is an extension of the empirical sciences rather than an abstract conceptual analysis. 
Wikipedia +1
His core contributions include: 
  • Critique of the Analytic-Synthetic Distinction: In his famous 1951 paper "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," Quine argued against the traditional divide between statements true purely by definition (analytic, e.g., "all bachelors are unmarried") and statements true by factual observation (synthetic). 
    YouTube·Bryan Van Norden +1
  • Confirmation Holism ("Web of Belief"): He proposed that our beliefs do not face empirical testing individually, but as a collective web. Any belief can be revised if necessary to accommodate new evidence. 
    YouTube·Philosophy Battle +1
  • Indeterminacy of Translation: In his seminal book Word and Object (1960), he argued that multiple, mutually incompatible translations of a language can exist while completely satisfying the exact same behavioral dispositions. 
    Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy +1

2. Quine (Computing)

In computer science, a quine is a computer program that takes no input and outputs an exact copy of its own source code. These self-replicating programs are a direct application of Kleene's recursion theorem and are possible in any Turing-complete programming language. 
Wikipedia
For example, a famous, short quine in Python looks like this:

s = 's = %r; print(s %% s)'; print(s % s)

The term "Quine" carries different meanings depending on whether you are looking at philosophy, computer science, or linguistics: 

1. Philosophy: "To Quine" (Verb)

In philosophical circles, the name has been turned into a verb. To "quine" means to deny the existence or significance of something that seems real or intuitive, or to show that a concept is fundamentally empty or useless. 
  • Origin: Named after philosopher W.V.O. Quine, who famously argued that concepts like "meaning" itself, or the distinction between analytical and synthetic truths, do not actually exist in the way we think they do. 

2. Computer Science: Self-Replication (Noun)

In computing, a quine is a program that prints its own source code without reading any external files or taking user input. 
  • The "Quine" Paradox: It is an algorithmic puzzle based on indirect self-reference. It mimics the paradox of a sentence describing its own construction. 

3. Linguistics: Quine's Paradox (Sentence)

In logic, a Quine sentence is a specific type of self-referential paradox that avoids using pronouns like "I" or "this sentence." 
  • Example: "yields falsehood when preceded by its quotation" yields falsehood when preceded by its quotation.
  • Meaning: If you take the phrase in quotes and place it in front of itself, the resulting sentence claims that it is false. This creates a logical loop similar to the famous Liar Paradox ("This statement is false").

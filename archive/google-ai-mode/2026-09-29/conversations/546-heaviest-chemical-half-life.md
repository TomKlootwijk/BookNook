### 1. The Standard Half-Life Derivation (First-Order Kinetics)

For standard elements, radioactive decay follows a first-order rate law. The mathematical derivation of half-life ( $t\_{1/2}$ ) is as follows: 

![](../assets/9a05b6f8eb450be9efa3.png)YouTube·easyedu +1

1. **Decay Law:** The number of remaining radioactive nuclei ( $N$ ) at time  $t$  depends on the initial amount ( $N\_{0}$ ) and the decay constant ( $\lambda $ ):  
    $N(t)=N\_{0}e^{-\lambda t}$
2. **Defining Half-Life:** By definition, at  $t = t\_{1/2}$ , exactly half of the sample remains ( $N = \frac{N\_0}{2}$ ). Substituting this yields:  
    $\frac{N\_{0}}{2}=N\_{0}e^{-\lambda t\_{1/2}}$
3. **Solving for  $t\_{1/2}$ :** Cancel  $N\_{0}$  and take the natural logarithm ( $\ln $ ) of both sides: 

   ![](../assets/9a05b6f8eb450be9efa3.png)YouTube·ZPhysics +1

     
    $\frac{1}{2}=e^{-\lambda t\_{1/2}}\implies \ln \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=-\lambda t\_{1/2}$   
    $-\ln (2)=-\lambda t\_{1/2}\implies t\_{1/2}=\frac{\ln (2)}{\lambda }\approx \frac{0.693}{\lambda }$

This classical derivation assumes  $\lambda $  is an independent, intrinsic constant. However, calculating  $\lambda $  from scratch for the heaviest elements reveals massive anomalies.

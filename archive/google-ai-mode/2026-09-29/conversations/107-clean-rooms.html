A cleanroom (or clean room) is an engineered, isolated environment designed to maintain an ultra-low concentration of airborne particulates, pollutants, and microbes. First invented in 1960 by Willis Whitfield, these spaces are heavily utilized in precision industries like semiconductor manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, biotechnology, aerospace, and medical device production. 
Wikipedia +4

How Cleanrooms Work

Cleanrooms continuously cycle and purify the air to eliminate microscopic particles using several synchronized mechanisms: 
  • HEPA/ULPA Filtration: High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) or Ultra-Low Penetration Air (ULPA) filters capture up to 99.97% or more of particles down to 0.3 microns. 
    YouTube·MEP Academy +1
  • Positive Air Pressure: Cleanrooms are typically kept at a higher atmospheric pressure than adjacent hallways. When a door opens, air rushes out rather than letting unfiltered outside air rush in. 
    ScienceDirect.com +3
  • Air Changes Per Hour (ACH): While a standard home changes its air a few times a day, a cleanroom can cycle its entire volume of air anywhere from 10 to 600+ times per hour depending on its classification. 
    YouTube·MEP Academy
  • Strict Protocols: Personnel act as the largest contamination source. They must pass through airlocks/gown rooms and wear specific Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) like bunny suits, hoods, gloves, and booties. 
    American Cleanroom Systems +1

ISO Classifications (ISO 14644-1)

Cleanrooms are classified by the maximum allowed number of particles per cubic meter of air. The scale runs from ISO 1 to ISO 9, where lower numbers represent cleaner environments:

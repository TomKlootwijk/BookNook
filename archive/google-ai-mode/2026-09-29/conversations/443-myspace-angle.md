The **"MySpace angle"** is a classic internet culture phenomenon from the mid-to-late 2000s, widely recognized as the **defining selfie technique** of the early social media era. 

![](../assets/07e659b8dad50a8dcd5c.png)google.com

It involves holding a digital camera or an early flip phone **high above the head** and tilting it downward at a sharp angle. This specific perspective achieved several visual effects that became universally popular among millennials, emo/scene subcultures, and early internet users:

* **Flattering Perspective:** The high, downward angle slims the jawline, emphasizes the eyes, and minimizes the appearance of a double chin.
* **Showcasing Hair:** It was perfectly optimized for displaying the voluminous, side-swept, or choppy "emo" hair trends of the era.
* **Hiding the Camera:** Because front-facing selfie cameras did not exist yet, users had to aim blindly while looking up into the lens, often extending their arm fully to get the framing right.

Today, the term is frequently referenced with nostalgic humor by millennials poking fun at their early internet profiles, or featured in period piece movies like the 2024 film [*Dìdi*](https://www.google.com/search?q=Didi+movie+2024) which meticulously recreates 2008 internet awkwardness.

## Response 2 — Google AI Mode

While the high angle was frequently used back then to create a slimming effect and disguise a double chin, the "MySpace angle" was actually a universal trend adopted by almost everyone on the platform, regardless of body type.

In the 2000s, phone cameras didn't have front-facing screens, so people had to guess their framing blindly. Holding the camera high up simply became the easiest, most popular way to get a clear, well-lit shot of your face and hair without cutting off half your head. It became the signature look of an entire generation's online profile pictures.

Today, selfie techniques have evolved significantly with front-facing cameras, studio lighting, and digital filters, making the old MySpace photo style mostly a nostalgic reminder of the early days of social media.

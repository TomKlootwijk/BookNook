To understand geometrically and ratio-wise why that 13 and the dot product formula work, you have to break the 3D volume down into a collection of infinitesimally thin geometric pyramids (or cones). 
Here is the step-by-step geometric breakdown of why the formula works exactly this way: 

1. The Geometry of a Single Pyramid

Think back to basic geometry: the volume of any pyramid or cone is given by: 
Volume=13×Base\ Area×Height
The 13 is a fixed geometric ratio that comes directly from calculus when you integrate a cross-sectional area that scales quadratically (r2) from a single point (the apex) out to a flat base. 

2. Measuring "Height" using Vectors

In the formula, we choose an arbitrary point in space as our origin (0,0,0). 
  • 𝐱 is the position vector pointing from the origin to a tiny piece of the surface area (dA).
  • 𝐧 is the unit normal vector pointing straight out, perpendicular to that surface piece dA. 
The "height" of the pyramid stretching from the origin to that surface patch is the perpendicular distance from the origin to the plane of dA. Geometrically, you project the position vector 𝐱 onto the normal vector 𝐧 using the dot product: 
Height=𝐱·𝐧

3. Summing Up the Infinitesimal Volumes

If you look at just one tiny patch of the surface dA, it forms the base of a tiny pyramid stretching back to the origin. Its tiny volume dV is: 
dV=13×dA⏟Base×(𝐱·𝐧)⏟Height
To find the total volume of the shape, you must add up the volumes of all these tiny pyramids across the entire surface. This continuous summation over a closed boundary is exactly what the closed surface integral (∮) does: 
V=∮dV=13∮∂V𝐱·𝐧dA

4. Why This Works for Any Shape (The Overlap Magic)

You might wonder: What if the origin is outside the shape, or the shape is hollow/irregular? 
  • Positive Volume: When the surface faces away from the origin, 𝐱·𝐧 is positive. The formula calculates the volume of the pyramid and adds it. 
  • Negative Volume: When the surface faces back toward the origin, 𝐱·𝐧 becomes negative (because the vectors point in opposite directions). The formula calculates that pyramid's volume and subtracts it. 
Geometrically, the negative volumes perfectly cancel out the empty space between the origin and the actual shape, leaving you with the exact volume of the object—no matter how complex or hollowed out it is!

The Ratio and Alignment Clash

When mathematicians began combining these symbols in the early 20th century, the visual conflict arose from how the characters are balanced relative to the text line: 
  • The standard single closed integral (∮): Because a single integral sign is tilted, the circle rests at an angle. It looks distinct from a Phi. 
  • The closed surface integral (∮∮ or ∮): Because a surface integral calculates a 2D boundary wrapper around a 3D volume, it requires a double integral.

To make the equations cleaner and avoid writing two giant crowbars with a circle stretched across them, publishers developed a shorthand notation where they use one single, perfectly upright, thick integral sign right down the dead center of the loop. 
Mathematics Stack Exchange
Because an uppercase Phi (Φ) is topologically constructed from the exact same typographic geometry—a central vertical stroke dividing a closed symmetric loop—compressing the closed surface integral into a compact shorthand makes it visually identical to a Phi, despite having a completely separate origin.

M.C. Escher frequently explored the concept of mathematical **infinity** through his art, most famously combining it with a **Möbius strip** in his iconic 1963 woodcut, Möbius Strip II (Red Ants).

A Möbius strip is a surface with only one side and one edge. Escher visually demonstrates this mind-bending property by depicting **nine red ants** crawling along a lattice-structured strip shaped like a figure-eight or **infinity sign (∞)**. Because a Möbius strip has no beginning or end, the ants are trapped walking in a perpetual loop, traveling on both what appears to be the "inside" and "outside" of the band without ever crossing an edge.

### Key Characteristics of Escher's Möbius Strips

* **Möbius Strip I (1961):** An earlier variation showcasing three flat, interlocking ribbons that twist around each other, highlighting the geometric properties rather than a continuous path.
* **Möbius Strip II (1963):** The classic "infinity" piece utilizing a lattice pattern, which allows the viewer to see through the strip and witness the ants crawling on the reverse side simultaneously.
* **The Infinity Connection:** By shaping the twisted band into a vertical figure-eight, Escher seamlessly married the mathematical symbol for infinity with a physical, three-dimensional object that embodies it.

### The Mathematical Behavior of the Ants

* **An Odd Number of Ants:** Escher deliberately placed exactly **nine ants** on the strip. Mathematically, using an odd number of equidistant travelers on a Möbius strip creates a specific symmetry—each ant is positioned directly opposite its "subterranean" counterpart on the other side of the porous lattice. If he had used an even number, they would form mirroring pairs. 

  ![](../assets/a1539faca41671f94284.jpg)Museum Escher in Het Paleis +1
* **The Perforated Lattice:** Escher chose to make the strip out of a **trellis-like, see-through material**. Because the band is porous, you can look straight through the holes and witness ants crawling right underneath each other simultaneously. 

  ![](../assets/4ac9c3d7ef01afd18e77.png)National Gallery of Art (.gov) +2
* **The Single-File March:** The ants travel in a perfect single-file procession. If you pick one specific ant and trace its path forward, you will watch it crawl along the entire length of the loop, smoothly transition to the "underside," and eventually return right back to where it started—**all without ever crossing over an edge**. 

  ![](../assets/9a05b6f8eb450be9efa3.png)YouTube·sabalero31 +1

Escher famously noted the irony of this structure, writing that while a normal ring-shaped band has two distinct surfaces, these ants are doomed to travel both the front and reverse sides in a state of endless, cyclical repetition.

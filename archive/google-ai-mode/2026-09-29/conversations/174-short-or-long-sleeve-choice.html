No, that is a misconception. Even if a nerve could be severed instantly—whether by a literal blade or metaphorically by a microscopic "molecular scissor" like CRISPR—it would still hurt significantly and would actually carry a very high risk of phantom pain. 
National Institutes of Health (NIH) | (.gov) +1
The speed of the cut does not prevent pain or its long-term neurological consequences. Here is why: 

1. The "Split Second" Signal Still Fires

Pain is not caused by the tool making the cut; it is caused by the electrical signal that the damaged nerve sends to your brain. 
  • The moment a nerve fiber is torn or severed, its cellular membrane ruptures.
  • This causes an immediate, massive influx of ions, generating a chaotic surge of electrical activity.
  • Even if the cut happens in a microsecond, that massive "injury discharge" travels up the spinal cord to the brain at speeds up to 250 miles per hour, registering as a sharp, intense burst of pain. 

2. Why it Triggers Phantom Pain

Phantom limb pain and chronic neuropathic pain do not happen because a cut was "too slow." They happen because the nerve was cut at all. 
  • Central Sensitization: The sudden explosion of distress signals from a severed nerve overwhelms the spinal cord, changing how it processes sensory data. The central nervous system essentially gets "stuck" in a hyper-reactive state. 
    National Institutes of Health (NIH) | (.gov)
  • Neuromas: When a peripheral nerve is cut, it tries to grow back. Because its original pathway is gone, the microscopic nerve fibers tangle into a sensitive clump called a neuroma. This neuroma continuously fires random, spontaneous pain signals to the brain. 
  • Cortical Remapping: The brain's map of the body expects input from that nerve. When the signal goes dead, the brain reorganizes itself. This mismatch between the brain's internal map and the missing physical input is a primary driver of phantom pain. 

🔬 What "Molecular Scissors" Actually Do

It sounds like you might be blending the concept of physical cutting with CRISPR-Cas9, which scientists frequently call "molecular scissors." 
YouTube·Henrik's Lab
CRISPR does not slice through macroscopic body parts or nerves; it cuts DNA strands inside a cell's nucleus to disable or fix a gene. Interestingly, researchers are currently using these molecular scissors to study pain management—not by physically cutting nerves, but by editing genes to permanently dial down how intensely nerves send pain signals.

The phrase "smallpox blankets" refers to one of the earliest and most infamous historical examples of attempted biological warfare, where blankets exposed to the smallpox virus were intentionally given to Native Americans. While smallpox is caused by a virus (Variola virus), your mention of "tiny little microbial bacteria and fungi" opens up the massive, fascinating world of the microbiome and environmental microbes—both the dangerous ones and the "good stuff" that keeps us alive. 
Here is a quick breakdown of how these different microscopic forces work: 

🦠 The Bad: Viruses & Historical Bioweapons

  • Smallpox (Variola): This is a virus, not a bacterium or fungus. It is a tiny package of genetic material that requires a host's cells to replicate. The most documented case of using blankets as a vector occurred during the Siege of Fort Pitt in 1763, where British officers gave smallpox-infected blankets to Delaware investigators. 
  • Other Historical Microbes: Unlike smallpox, other historical bioweapons were bacteria, such as Bacillus anthracis (anthrax) or Yersinia pestis (the plague). 

🧫 The "Good Stuff": Bacteria and Fungi

While some microbes cause disease, the vast majority of bacteria and fungi are essential for life on Earth. 
  • The Human Microbiome: Your body is home to trillions of "good" bacteria and fungi. They live mostly in your gut, skin, and mouth. They digest food, synthesize vitamins (like Vitamin K), and train your immune system to fight off the actual bad guys. 
  • Fungi (The Recyclers): Environmental fungi and molds break down dead organic matter, returning vital nutrients to the soil. Without them, forests would be piled high with dead wood. 
  • Fermentation: We rely on good bacteria (like Lactobacillus) and fungi (like Saccharomyces cerevisiae, or yeast) to make bread, cheese, yogurt, beer, and kimchi. 
  • Medicine: Strangely enough, we use the "good stuff" to fight the bad stuff. The world's first modern antibiotic, penicillin, was derived from a common house fungus (Penicillium rubens).

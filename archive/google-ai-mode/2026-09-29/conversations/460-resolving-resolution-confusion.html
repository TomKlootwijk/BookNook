The term RK3 and RK4 you mentioned earlier refers to Runge-Kutta methods (specifically 3rd-order and 4th-order), which are famous mathematical algorithms used in computer simulations, physics engines, and numerical analysis to solve differential equations—not display resolutions! 
The Classical Fourth-Order Runge-Kutta method (RK4) is the most widely used variation. It approximates how a system changes over time by taking a weighted average of four distinct steps (slopes) across an interval h. 

The RK4 Formula

To solve an ordinary differential equation dydt=f(t,y), the next value yn+1 is computed from the current value yn using: 
yn+1=yn+16h(k1+2k2+2k3+k4)
Where the four trial steps are calculated as follows: 
  • k₁ (Slope at the beginning): f(tn,yn)
  • k₂ (Slope at the midpoint using k₁): f(tn+h2,yn+hk12)
  • k₃ (Slope at the midpoint using k₂): f(tn+h2,yn+hk22)
  • k₄ (Slope at the end using k₃): f(tn+h,yn+hk3) 

Visual Intuition

Instead of just shooting straight forward blindly (like the simpler Euler's method), RK4 samples the slope at the start, two variations in the middle, and one at the end of the time step. By weighting the middle steps heavier (26 each vs 16 for the endpoints), it drastically reduces error. 

RK3 vs. RK4

  • RK3 (Third-Order): Evaluates 3 slopes per step. Its local truncation error is on the order of 𝒪(h4).
  • RK4 (Fourth-Order): Evaluates 4 slopes per step. Its local truncation error is significantly smaller, on the order of 𝒪(h5), making it the industry standard for general simulations (like game physics or planetary orbits) because it balances speed and high precision perfectly.

Your metaphor captures a harsh, cynical view of human worth, but it perfectly mirrors a documented psychological phenomenon known as prototype theory and appearance bias. The Psychology of the "Perfect Prototype"

In cognitive science, our brains rely on an idealized prototype—a mental blueprint of what a specific object "should" look like. 
ScienceDirect.com
  • The Supermarket Standard: Mainstream grocery chains train consumers to expect perfectly symmetrical, vibrant fruits and vegetables. Anything knobby, multi-legged, or discolored is filtered out by suppliers before it ever reaches the shelf.

Where the "Failed Prototypes" Actually Go

While your prompt suggests they just get thrown away, the global food system (and human society) has had to find creative ways to handle things that don't fit the standard mold: 
DestinationWhat Happens to the "Imperfect" Item
The Discount BoxRetailers market them under self-deprecating labels like "Ugly But Delicious" or "Imperfect" to appeal to bargain hunters.
Specialty ChannelsServices like Misfits Market or pop-up concepts like Utrecht's Crooked Supermarket intentionally curate these exact "failed prototypes" for consumers who value substance over symmetry.
Processing & PureeingDeformed produce is often chopped up, juiced, or pureed. Once a knobby carrot is blended into a soup or a bruised apple is turned into sauce, its lack of an "aesthetically pleasing look" becomes completely irrelevant.
Ultimately, whether dealing with produce or people, the bias toward an "idealized prototype" often causes society to discard or overlook immense value simply because it isn't packaged in a standard, symmetrical box.

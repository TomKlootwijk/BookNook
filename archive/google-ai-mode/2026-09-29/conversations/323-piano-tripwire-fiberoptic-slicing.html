Response 1 · Google AI Mode
Neither piano wire nor fiber-optic cable functions as an effective "slicing" tripwire. In fiction or tactical tropes, thin wires are sometimes depicted as hazardous slicing hazards, but physics and material science contradict this utility in reality: 
  • Piano Wire: High-tensile carbon steel makes an exceptionally strong and rigid tripwire for triggering structural traps, but it lacks the velocity and kinetic energy necessary to "slice" through an object or a person just by being stretched across a path. 
    FliteTest Forum
  • Fiber-Optic Cable: Made of flexible glass or plastic cores, fiber-optic cables are designed entirely for data transmission. They possess no structural sharpness, are relatively brittle when exposed to sheer force, and break easily when subjected to high tension. 
    YouTube·Fiber Instrument Sales +1

The Reality of Fiber-Optic Splicing

The term "slicing" is often a linguistic confusion with splicing. In telecommunications, fiber-optic cable is never cut rudimentarily. Instead, technicians use a precision tool called a cleaver to score and snap the glass cleanly at a perfect 90-degree angle. They then use a fusion splicer to melt the microscopic glass cores together using an electric arc to ensure a seamless data path. 
YouTube·Fiber Instrument Sales +2
Similarly, in instrument repair, piano wires are "spliced" by interlocking tightly twisted loops to mend a broken string without replacing the entire coil.

Response 2 · Google AI Mode
No, HEPA filters are not made of wires, and there is a common double-mixup happening here with the terms. 
First, the actual term is HEPA (High-Efficiency Particulate Air). People often confuse it with HIPAA, which is a United States healthcare privacy law regarding patient data security. 
Wikipedia +1
Second, the filter itself is not a grid of little metal wires. It is a dense, paper-like mat made of millions of randomly tangled microscopic fibers (usually fiberglass, polyester, or polypropylene). 
Ossila +1

Fibers vs. Wires: How HEPA Filters Work

If a filter were made of a wire grid, it would act like a kitchen sieve—particles smaller than the holes would pass right through. HEPA filters do not work like sieves. Because they use a dense maze of microscopic fibers rather than a straight grid, they trap particles using completely different physics: 
www.tion.co.uk +3
  • Interception: Medium particles follow the air stream but get close enough to a fiber that they graze it and get stuck. 
    www.tion.co.uk +1
  • Impaction: Large particles are too heavy to navigate the twists and turns of the air stream, so they fly straight ahead and slam directly into a fiber. 
    www.tion.co.uk
  • Diffusion: The smallest particles (like viruses and smoke) bounce around erratically in a zig-zag pattern because they collide with air molecules. This chaos makes them highly likely to wander into a fiber and get trapped. 
    www.tion.co.uk
(Note: Some industrial air purifiers do use a stiff "wire mesh" or galvanized steel frame on the outside just to hold the soft, folded HEPA paper in place, but the actual filtering material is entirely fiber.)
